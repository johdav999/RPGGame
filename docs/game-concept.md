# THE SHATTERED SEA

*A quantum-fantasy RPG world concept*

Imagine a world of thousands of islands scattered across an immense ocean. Kingdoms rise and fall, merchant fleets connect distant cultures, and ancient ruins stand on remote cliffs and beneath the waves.

The inhabitants live in a world resembling the late medieval or early Renaissance period. They build sailing ships, forge swords, wage wars, worship gods, and practice primitive sciences.

But scattered throughout the islands are remnants of something far older and vastly more advanced.

And recently, something has begun to awaken.

## 1. The world: The Shattered Sea

The known world consists of several great archipelagos separated by dangerous stretches of ocean.

No one knows whether a continent exists beyond the horizon.

The islands vary dramatically. Some support powerful kingdoms, others are volcanic wastelands, dense jungles, frozen mountains, or isolated settlements that have had little contact with the outside world.

What makes this world unusual is the presence of ancient structures.

### The Monoliths

Enormous structures of unknown material, apparently immune to corrosion and physical damage.

### The Drowned Cities

Vast architectural complexes beneath the sea, some containing machinery that still functions.

### The Relics

Small objects that can alter matter, heal injuries, distort space, or produce inexplicable effects.

### The Gates

Ancient installations found on distant islands. Most are dormant, but a few have recently activated.

These structures predate all known civilizations.

Some are revered as sacred places. Others are feared and avoided.

There is no universally accepted explanation for their origin.

## 2. The vanished civilization: The Architects

Thousands of years ago, an extraordinarily advanced civilization existed across the islands.

The Architects had discovered something fundamental about the universe.

Reality is not a single fixed sequence of events. It is a vast collection of possible states, governed by probabilities.

They learned to manipulate these states through technologies that could maintain quantum coherence at extraordinary scales.

Their achievements were almost unimaginable.

They could alter the properties of matter, transfer objects between distant locations, accelerate biological regeneration, and influence the probability of physical events.

Their cities required little conventional energy. Their ships could traverse oceans without sails. Disease and famine had largely disappeared.

But they discovered something else.

The universe contained more possibilities than their understanding could safely accommodate.

They began experimenting with increasingly unlikely states of reality, attempting to overcome limitations that had previously seemed absolute.

Eventually, they built an interconnected network of installations across the islands: the Lattice.

Its purpose was to stabilize reality while allowing increasingly powerful manipulations.

Then, within a remarkably short period, their civilization disappeared.

There are no reliable records of a great war or natural catastrophe. Many ancient cities appear to have been abandoned in the middle of ordinary activities.

Some inhabitants seem simply to have vanished.

Others left behind objects, messages, and structures that make no sense.

The truth of what happened is one of the game’s central mysteries.

## 3. The Gifted: humans who can influence reality

A small percentage of people are born with unusual abilities.

They are known by different names throughout the islands: the Gifted, Weavers, the Touched, or the Unbound.

Most can perform only minor feats.

A fisherman might instinctively know where a fish will appear. A warrior might survive encounters through apparently impossible luck. A healer might accelerate the recovery of an injured person.

Rare individuals can accomplish much more.

The underlying explanation is biological. The Architects altered certain human populations to interact with the Lattice through specialized neural structures.

These modifications survived in descendants long after the civilization disappeared.

However, the abilities are neither unlimited nor completely understood.

### How the abilities work

The Gifted do not produce supernatural energy. Instead, their minds interact with the Lattice, which provides the physical means to influence possible outcomes.

Their effectiveness depends on three factors:

- **Coherence:** Their ability to maintain a stable connection to the Lattice.
- **Probability distance:** How unlikely the desired outcome would normally be.
- **Environmental resonance:** The strength of the Lattice in their immediate surroundings.

A small manipulation might be easy. A major alteration of reality could overwhelm the user, cause severe neurological damage, or destabilize the surrounding environment.

The Gifted are therefore powerful but vulnerable.

And because most societies do not understand their abilities, they are often feared, exploited, worshipped, or persecuted.

## 4. The awakening: something is wrong with reality

For centuries, the ancient installations remained largely dormant.

Then, approximately twenty years before the game begins, strange events started occurring.

At first, they seemed unrelated.

Ships vanished from familiar trade routes. Entire villages experienced the same dreams. Animals were born with impossible anatomical features.

In certain places, time seemed to behave strangely.

Then the first Fracture appeared.

A fishing settlement on a remote island disappeared during the night. When a trading vessel arrived several days later, the buildings remained intact, but the inhabitants were gone.

The sea around the island was unnaturally still.

Objects occasionally moved without being touched. Footsteps could be heard inside empty houses.

And in the central square stood a figure resembling a human being, motionless, its outline constantly shifting.

The sailors who returned described something that should not have been possible.

The figure appeared to exist in several positions simultaneously.

The event became known as the First Unraveling.

Since then, the phenomena have spread.

## 5. The antagonist: The Unmaking

I would avoid making the central evil simply another powerful wizard, demon, or ancient god.

Instead, the threat could emerge directly from the Architects’ manipulation of reality.

The Architects discovered that the Lattice could access alternative possible states. But when they pushed beyond the limits of stable physical reality, they encountered a phenomenon they could neither understand nor control.

They called it the Unmaking.

It is not necessarily a conscious being in the human sense.

It is a self-propagating instability: a process that breaks down the distinction between mutually exclusive possibilities.

Normally, an object has one definite macroscopic state. Under the influence of the Unmaking, incompatible states begin to coexist.

A person might be simultaneously alive and dead. A building might exist in two different configurations. A location might connect to several geographically distant places.

The Unmaking spreads by destabilizing the Lattice.

And it is accelerating.

### The creatures of the Unmaking

#### The Echoes

Fragments of people existing in multiple incompatible states. Some are hostile; others seem confused or desperate.

#### The Fractured

Animals and humans physically transformed by unstable matter. Their bodies constantly reorganize.

#### The Nullborn

Entities that appear to be stable manifestations of the Unmaking. They can suppress quantum abilities.

#### The Fracture Zones

Areas where the laws of physics become unreliable. Geography, gravity, time, and causality may behave unpredictably.

One especially interesting possibility is that the Unmaking gradually develops intelligence.

Not because it was originally alive, but because it absorbs information, memories, and patterns from everything it encounters.

Eventually, it begins communicating.

And what it says suggests that the Architects may not have been innocent victims.

## 6. The societies and their conflicting interpretations

Different cultures interpret the ancient technology and the Unmaking in radically different ways.

| Faction | Belief and motivation |
| --- | --- |
| The Maritime Crown | A powerful island kingdom seeking to control ancient artifacts for military and economic advantage |
| The Order of the Veil | A religious organization that considers the Gifted dangerous and the Fractures a divine punishment |
| The Cartographers’ Guild | Explorers and scholars mapping ancient ruins, anomalies, and forgotten sea routes |
| The Keepers | A secretive order preserving fragments of the Architects’ knowledge |
| The Ascendants | A movement convinced that the Unmaking represents humanity’s next stage of evolution |

None of these factions needs to be entirely good or evil.

The Maritime Crown might be authoritarian but provide stability. The Order of the Veil could persecute the Gifted while correctly recognizing that excessive manipulation accelerates the catastrophe.

The Ascendants might initially appear to be dangerous fanatics, but some of their discoveries could be genuine.

This allows the player to make difficult choices without obvious moral answers.

## 7. The player’s journey

I would begin the game on a relatively ordinary island, far from the major kingdoms.

The player could choose their background: sailor, mercenary, scholar, smuggler, healer, or an individual with latent quantum abilities.

The opening hours establish a recognizable world of settlements, political rivalries, dangerous wildlife, and maritime trade.

Then an ancient artifact activates.

The player becomes involved in an event that reveals a connection between their own abilities, the Lattice, and the spreading Fractures.

The story could develop through four acts.

- **Act I — The First Fracture**

  Investigate disappearances and strange phenomena across a small island group. Discover an ancient installation and awaken an ability that should not exist.

- **Act II — The Forgotten Network**

  Travel between kingdoms and remote islands, recover lost knowledge, build alliances, and discover that the ancient installations form a connected system beneath the ocean.

- **Act III — The Architects’ Legacy**

  Learn what destroyed the ancient civilization. Discover that the Lattice was not merely a technological network but a containment system, and that its failure is releasing something the Architects deliberately imprisoned.

- **Act IV — The Choice of Reality**

  Reach the central installation and decide whether to restore the Lattice, destroy it, or fundamentally change its purpose. Each choice transforms the future of the islands.

## 8. Make the archipelago central to gameplay

The island setting should be more than a backdrop.

It provides a natural structure for exploration, discovery, and progression.

Players could acquire and upgrade ships, hire crews, navigate dangerous waters, establish trading relationships, and discover remote islands.

Different regions could have distinct relationships with the Lattice.

For example, one island might have a strong resonance that makes quantum abilities exceptionally powerful. Another could be a dead zone where they barely function.

Some islands might shift position during Fracture events. Others could become accessible only when ancient gates activate.

A particularly interesting mechanic would be changing sea routes.

As the Unmaking spreads, previously safe routes become dangerous, while entirely new passages appear. This could affect trade, warfare, exploration, and regional politics.

It also provides a natural way to make the world evolve as the story progresses.

## 9. The central mystery — a possible twist

There is one idea I think could make this world substantially more distinctive.

What if the Architects did not disappear because they failed to control the Unmaking? What if they deliberately removed themselves from reality to contain it?

The Lattice was designed to keep multiple possible realities separated.

But the Architects discovered that their experiments had created a self-sustaining instability capable of spreading throughout the world.

To prevent catastrophe, they sacrificed their own civilization, locking themselves and the instability into a separate quantum state.

The monuments and artifacts left behind are components of the containment system.

The Gifted were deliberately created as biological interfaces, intended to maintain and eventually repair the network.

Over thousands of years, the containment system has deteriorated.

And now the Unmaking is returning.

But the player eventually discovers a further complication.

The Unmaking contains the surviving consciousnesses of billions of Architects, trapped in a state of endless instability.

Restoring the containment would save the present world but condemn those consciousnesses indefinitely.

Destroying the containment could release them, but might also destroy the world.

And a third option — reconstructing the Lattice — could allow both civilizations to survive, although the outcome is deeply uncertain.

This makes the central conflict more than a battle against evil.

It becomes a question about sacrifice, identity, consciousness, and the nature of reality.

## 10. One design decision I would make early

I would keep the world visually and culturally grounded in traditional fantasy, while making the underlying explanation scientific.

Ordinary people use swords, bows, sailing ships, and conventional medicine. Most have never seen a functioning ancient artifact.

Quantum manipulation is rare, difficult, and dangerous. The ancient technology should feel mysterious rather than familiar science fiction.

The player initially experiences the world as fantasy, but gradually discovers that its supernatural elements follow a consistent set of rules.

And importantly, not everything should be explained immediately. Even by the end, some aspects of the Architects’ discoveries could remain beyond human understanding.

The result could combine the exploration and world-building of The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind, the factional choices of Baldur’s Gate 3, and the archaeological mystery of Outer Wilds, while establishing a distinct identity.

The biggest creative choice is the nature of the Unmaking: should it be an impersonal cosmic phenomenon that humans interpret as evil, or an actual intelligence with intentions, plans, and the ability to manipulate people? I would lean toward starting with the former and gradually revealing the latter.
